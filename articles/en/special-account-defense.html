Japan's One-Time Defense Funding Scheme — The Special Law That Extracted ¥1.84 Trillion from Special Accounts, and the Tomahawks Bought at Twice the US Navy Price

The "amount first, funding source later" structure starting from the May 2022 Japan-US summit. A new law extracted ¥1.84 trillion from Special Accounts and channeled 80% directly into US-made weapons via FMS. We trace the structure through primary sources from both Japanese and US governments.

1. Introduction — Where Did ¥1.84 Trillion Come From, and Where Did It Go?

In FY2023 (Reiwa 5, April 2023 – March 2024), the Japanese government transferred a one-time ¥1.84 trillion from the Foreign Exchange Fund Special Account (FEFSA) and the Fiscal Investment and Loan Program (FILP) Special Account into the general account, and from there channeled the funds toward defense spending. This was a "one-time, single-event scheme" with no precedent in prior years.

The headlines at the time used the framing of "increasing the defense budget by raising taxes." But the actual reality of how the money moved was different: funds were extracted from Special Accounts before any tax increase took effect. Article 14 of the Defense Funding Securing Act locks the use of these funds to defense spending, and a new "Defense Capabilities Enhancement Fund" was established inside the general account — effectively functioning as a quasi-defense special account.

And those funds did not stop at defense procurement broadly. Approximately 80% of FY2023 defense procurement (¥1.4768 trillion) was directed into US-made weapons via Foreign Military Sales (FMS), with Japan paying Tomahawk unit prices approximately 1.9–2.3× the US Navy's domestic price. Furthermore, FMS prepayments are held in the FMS Trust Fund at the US Treasury, where they are invested in US Treasury securities and similar instruments.

This article traces the structure of where ¥1.84 trillion came from and where it ended up, using primary sources from both Japanese and US governments — the Ministry of Finance, Ministry of Defense, Cabinet Secretariat, parliamentary records, Board of Audit reports, US DSCA Congressional Notifications, the US Navy budget book, and others.

Note for international readers: Japan's Special Accounts (特別会計) are separate fiscal pockets — 19 in total across the central government — that operate alongside the general account. Unlike the general account, which resets annually under the single-year budget principle, Special Accounts maintain fund balances that roll over year to year, accumulating into trillion-yen-scale reserves. The Foreign Exchange Fund Special Account (FEFSA) primarily holds foreign currency assets used for exchange-rate intervention, while the Fiscal Investment and Loan Program (FILP) Special Account funds long-term policy lending. Both are central to this article.

2. Anatomy of the ¥1.84 Trillion — The Three Pathways

The ¥1.84 trillion flowed into the general account through three separate routes. Each had a different legal basis. The Ministry of Finance's official budget documents reveal the structure clearly:

① Foreign Exchange Fund Special Account (FEFSA) → ¥1,200,433,043,000

The largest route. Article 3 of the Defense Funding Securing Act authorized a special transfer from the FEFSA's "surplus" to the general account.

📜 LAW / Defense Funding Securing Act, Article 3

"In FY2023, in addition to the transfer to the general account as stipulated in Article 8, Paragraph 2 of the Foreign Exchange Fund Special Account Act (Act No. 56 of 1951), an amount equivalent to the surplus calculated according to specified methods may be transferred to the general account."

— Source: Defense Funding Securing Act (Act No. 69 of 2023)

The FEFSA's fund balance at the end of FY2022 was approximately ¥197 trillion, the majority held as foreign currency assets including US Treasury securities. Investment income (interest, dividends, exchange gains) accumulates within the FEFSA. Under the standard rule, surplus transfers to the general account are calculated by a fixed formula. The Defense Funding Securing Act overrode this, enabling a discretionary transfer of ¥1.2 trillion — approximately three times the typical annual transfer.

② FILP Special Account, Investment Account → ¥436.7 Billion

The second route involved the Investment Account (also known as the Industrial Investment Account) of the FILP Special Account. Funds came from the surplus of returns on investments in policy-oriented entities (JBIC, Nippon Export and Investment Insurance, etc.).

This route was authorized through the regular FY2023 budget rather than the Defense Funding Securing Act itself, but with explicit Cabinet-level coordination that this surplus would be earmarked for defense.

③ FILP Special Account, Fiscal Loan Fund Account → ¥200 Billion

The third route. Article 2 of the Defense Funding Securing Act authorized a one-time transfer of ¥200 billion from the Fiscal Loan Fund Account.

📜 LAW / Defense Funding Securing Act, Article 2

"In FY2023, ¥200 billion shall be transferred from the Fiscal Loan Fund Account of the Fiscal Investment and Loan Program Special Account to the general account."

— Source: Defense Funding Securing Act, Article 2

The Fiscal Loan Fund Account is the largest of the FILP Special Account's three accounts, holding ¥126 trillion in fund balance (as of end-FY2024). Its standard purpose is funding long-term loans to JBIC, Japan Bank for International Cooperation, and other policy-financial institutions. Diverting ¥200 billion from this account to defense spending is itself outside the original mission of the account, and it required a special law to authorize.

3. Locked to Defense Use by Law — Article 14 of the Defense Funding Securing Act

Once transferred to the general account, the ¥1.84 trillion was not freely usable. Article 14 of the Defense Funding Securing Act stipulates:

📜 LAW / Defense Funding Securing Act, Article 14

"The funds transferred to the general account pursuant to the provisions of Articles 2 and 3 shall be used to cover expenditures related to drastic strengthening of defense capabilities (excluding expenditures financed by national bonds)."

— Source: Defense Funding Securing Act, Article 14

This is unusual. Under Japan's general account budget principle (single-year, no use restriction), funds in the general account are fungible — they can be allocated to any expenditure category based on each year's Cabinet decision and Diet approval. Article 14 violates this principle: the use of these specific funds is fixed to defense expenditures by law.

"Defense Capabilities Enhancement Fund" — A Quasi-Defense Special Account Inside the General Account

The Defense Funding Securing Act also established a new fund within the general account: the Defense Capabilities Enhancement Fund (防衛力強化資金, Article 7).

This fund operates as follows:

  • Surplus revenues earmarked for defense (FEFSA transfers, investment account transfers, asset sales, etc.) are deposited into this fund
  • Funds are drawn from this account for defense expenditures as needed across multiple fiscal years
  • Unlike the general account, the balance can roll over across fiscal years
  • Defense Capabilities Enhancement Fund operates essentially like a Special Account dedicated to defense

⚠️ Interpretation Conceptually, the Defense Capabilities Enhancement Fund creates a fiscal vehicle that has the rollover characteristics of a Special Account, while remaining nominally inside the general account. This sidesteps the political and legal complexity of creating a formal "Defense Special Account" (which has been discussed and rejected for decades in Japanese fiscal policy) while delivering the same functional effect.

4. Timeline — Amount Was Decided First, Funding Source Was Sought Later from Special Accounts

Reviewing the chronological order of decisions reveals the structural problem clearly: the total defense budget amount (¥43 trillion over 5 years) was decided first, and the funding source was figured out afterward by reaching into Special Accounts.

The pattern is clear: the ¥43 trillion / 5-year defense budget total was decided at the Cabinet level in December 2022, before the Defense Funding Securing Act was even submitted to the Diet. The financial scheme to fund this — Special Account transfers, the Defense Capabilities Enhancement Fund — was then legislated retroactively to match the predetermined amount.

The constitutional principle of fiscal democracy (Article 83 of the Japanese Constitution: "The authority to administer national finances shall be exercised as the Diet shall determine") suggests that the Diet should determine the amount based on examined need. In this case, the order was reversed.

6. Destination of ¥1.84 Trillion (Part 1) — FMS Buying Spree of US-Made Weapons

Where did the ¥1.84 trillion actually go? The answer, traced through the FY2023 defense budget detailed line items, is clear: the majority went to procurement, and the majority of that procurement went to US-made weapons via Foreign Military Sales (FMS).

FY2023 FMS procurement total: ¥1.4768 trillion. The previous year (FY2022) was ¥361.7 billion — meaning FMS procurement quadrupled year-over-year.

Looking at the 10-year trend: in FY2013, Japan's FMS procurement was ¥117 billion. By FY2023, it had reached ¥1.4768 trillion — a 13× expansion in a decade.

If we apply a rough breakdown — assuming approximately 80% of FY2023 procurement was FMS-related — then of the ¥1.84 trillion extracted from Special Accounts, approximately ¥1.47 trillion (80%) went directly into US-made weapons. The remaining 20% covered Japanese domestic equipment, base maintenance, and personnel costs.

⚠️ Inference The strict 80% breakdown is approximate. The ¥1.84T Special Account transfer was deposited into the Defense Capabilities Enhancement Fund and drawn down across multiple fiscal years. The exact correspondence between "which yen from the SA transfer went to which procurement" is not separately accounted in MOD budget documents. The 80% figure reflects the proportion of FY2023's overall procurement expenditure that was FMS, not a strict allocation of the SA transfer.

7. Tomahawk Unit Prices — Japan Pays Approximately Twice the US Navy's Domestic Price

The Tomahawk cruise missile contract signed in January 2024 (400 missiles for ¥254 billion) became a focal point because the unit price comparison revealed a striking pattern. The Japanese government refused to disclose the specific unit cost — Prime Minister Kishida stated at a February 2024 Diet committee that "we did not specify" the unit price.

However, the contract total and quantity are public, allowing back-calculation. Cross-referencing with the US Navy's internal procurement budget reveals a substantial price differential.

① The Japan unit price is approximately 1.9–2.3× the US Navy's domestic price. Even using the more conservative DSCA-basis ($530M/unit), the multiplier is ~1.9×. ② Australia, also a FMS purchaser, pays a price between the US Navy's and Japan's. Australia's ~¥410M/unit is approximately 1.5× the US Navy domestic price. Japan's price exceeds Australia's by 30-55%, suggesting that Japan-specific factors (volume, timing, support packages, foreign currency exposure) account for the additional spread.

③ The body-only unit price for Japan is treated as "non-disclosed by the government." Akahata reported that Kishida, when asked at the February 2024 Diet committee, "did not disclose" the body unit cost. Japan's standard FMS contracts include lifecycle support packages, training, and spares — these are bundled and the breakdown is not made public. Whether the high spread reflects bundling overhead or actual body-price discrimination cannot be determined from public data.

8. Destination of ¥1.84 Trillion (Part 2) — Prepayment to the FMS Trust Fund at the US Treasury

The Foreign Military Sales (FMS) system requires the purchasing country to prepay 100% of the contract amount before equipment delivery. These prepayments do not stay with the manufacturer (Raytheon for Tomahawks, Lockheed Martin for F-35, etc.). Instead, they are held in the FMS Trust Fund at the US Treasury, invested in US Treasury securities and similar instruments.

Payment Flow (Based on Japan's Board of Audit FY2018 Report)

Japan's Board of Audit conducted a special investigation of FMS procurement procedures based on a 2018 request from the House of Councillors. The flow is:

  1. Japan signs an LOA (Letter of Offer and Acceptance) with the US Defense Security Cooperation Agency (DSCA)
  2. Japan's Ministry of Defense remits the full contract amount in USD to the FMS Trust Fund at the US Treasury
  3. The funds are invested in US Treasury securities and similar instruments, generating interest
  4. When manufacturer payments come due (typically over multiple years), funds are drawn from the trust fund and paid to the manufacturer
  5. Interest earned during the holding period accrues to the US Treasury, not to Japan
  6. After delivery and acceptance, any unused funds (surplus) should theoretically be returned to Japan via the Final Statement Account process

📄 Primary Source Board of Audit of Japan, FMS Investigation Report (FY2018), Section 2

US Treasury Investment Structure — Japan's Prepayments Used to Buy US Treasury Securities

The structural irony of this arrangement is worth pausing on:

  • Japan's FEFSA already holds ~¥187 trillion in foreign currency assets, the majority in US Treasury securities (the asset that finances US fiscal deficits)
  • Japan now withdraws ¥1.84 trillion from this FEFSA and transfers it via Special Law to the general account
  • The general account uses this for defense procurement, ~80% of which (¥1.48 trillion) goes to FMS
  • FMS prepayments are held in the FMS Trust Fund at the US Treasury
  • The FMS Trust Fund invests in US Treasury securities

The structural cycle: Japanese taxpayer funds → FEFSA holdings of US Treasury securities → withdrawn via Special Law → reinvested in US Treasury securities via the FMS Trust Fund.

Unsettled Balances — A 30-Year Problem Repeatedly Flagged by Japan's Board of Audit

Since 1997, Japan's Board of Audit has repeatedly flagged "uncollected balances" in the FMS Trust Fund. The most recent reports note:

🇯🇵 Japan / Board of Audit FY2018 Report

"Among items requiring reconciliation (where equipment has been delivered but final settlement has not been completed), the total amount unreconciled exceeded ¥1.14 trillion that has remained uncollected for five or more years."

"The Board of Audit has expressed concerns about unreconciled balances in every report since FY2002. Despite improvements in administrative procedures, the structural problem of multi-year delays in final settlement persists."

— Source: Board of Audit of Japan, FY2018 Audit Report (FMS Procurement Investigation)

More recently, Nikkei reported in January 2026:

🇯🇵 Japan / Nikkei, January 2026

"As of the end of FY2024 (March 2025), approximately ¥1 trillion in FMS procurement remains unreconciled (uncollected) for more than five years past the originally scheduled delivery time. While FMS procurement has expanded sharply since FY2023 under the Defense Capabilities Enhancement Fund framework, settlement and reconciliation processes have not kept pace."

— Source: Nikkei, "Outstanding US Equipment Procurement Reaches ¥1 Trillion Over 5 Years" (January 16, 2026)

⚠️ Note The ¥1 trillion "uncollected after 5+ years" figure represents items where equipment was either not delivered on the originally scheduled timeline or where final accounting reconciliation has not been completed. This does not necessarily mean ¥1 trillion was lost — it means ¥1 trillion sits in the US Treasury's trust fund (earning interest for the US) while not yet being closed out from Japan's accounting perspective.

9. Remaining Questions

Several questions about the ¥1.84 trillion scheme cannot yet be answered from public information.

① The FY2024 and beyond pattern. The ¥1.84 trillion in FY2023 was a one-time transfer authorized by the Defense Funding Securing Act. But the Defense Capabilities Enhancement Fund created by the same Act is a recurring fiscal vehicle. How much additional capital is being routed into it in FY2024, FY2025, and FY2026 — and from which sources — requires tracking each year's budget documents.

② Final settlement of the Tomahawk contract. The 400-unit, ¥254 billion contract signed in January 2024 is scheduled for delivery from 2025 through 2027. As deliveries are completed, the Board of Audit framework requires reconciliation. Whether the actual delivered unit price (after settlement) matches or differs from the contract amount will be a key data point. This will be visible in approximately 2026-2028 audit reports.

③ FEFSA investment income transparency. The Defense Funding Securing Act used FEFSA "surplus" — that is, investment income on foreign currency assets — for the ¥1.2 trillion transfer. Japan's FEFSA is governed by transparency rules that are stricter than they were in the 1990s but still less detailed than equivalent reserves elsewhere. The methodology for calculating "surplus available for transfer" should be made more transparent.

④ The 30-year history of unreconciled FMS balances. The Board of Audit has flagged this issue since 1997. The cumulative total of unreconciled amounts attributable to Japan (not just the current snapshot) would be a useful longitudinal data point. We plan to compile this from individual annual reports in a future article.

⑤ The Defense Capabilities Enhancement Fund's year-by-year balance. Since the Fund operates with rollover capability, its balance at the end of each fiscal year would reveal whether the system is functioning as a "savings vehicle" for defense (accumulating funds) or as a "pass-through" vehicle (spent down each year). This data is published in MOF settlement documents but requires aggregation across multiple years to interpret.

These five questions form the agenda for follow-up articles in the Fiscal OSINT Japan "US-Bound Public Capital Flows" series.
