Yokohama Flower Expo ¥95.3B ── The Structure of Spending Other People's Money on Other People

When public funds become careless: an anatomy of a project labeled "public benefit"

Fiscal OSINT Japan / June 4, 2026

On March 19, 2027, the "2027 International Horticultural Expo (GREEN×EXPO 2027)" will open at the former Kamiseya Communication Facility in Asahi-ku and Seya-ku, Yokohama. Spanning 242 hectares of land returned by the US military in 2015, it will be the highest-tier A1-class horticultural exposition certified by the International Association of Horticultural Producers (AIPH).

The project budget stands at ¥95.3 billion (as of December 2025). This will be Japan's first A1-class horticultural expo in 37 years since the 1990 Osaka Flower Expo. The official aims include "achieving the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)," "realizing a green society," and "spreading Japanese horticultural culture worldwide."

But of this ¥95.3 billion, only ¥41.7 billion (the venue construction cost) is firmly secured. The remaining ¥53.6 billion (operating costs) depends on yet-to-be-realized projected income—¥41.8 billion from admission fees and ¥11.8 billion from other sources. Not a single yen of this has been collected yet.

And the basis for that ¥41.8 billion was something an opposition lawmaker had questioned in the Diet four years ago, saying "the foundation needs to be more solid."

This article examines the structure of this ¥95.3 billion project, drawing from the 175 contract records, financial statements, Diet proceedings, and Yokohama City Council records of the Japan Association for the International Horticultural Expo 2027, Yokohama (the operating body, a public interest incorporated association).

All verification data is available on the companion dashboard at https://nana6fu.github.io/fiscal-osint-japan/hanahaku.html

1How ¥95.3 Billion Grew

The project budget has grown in three stages from the conceptual phase:

  • 2018 Conceptual Phase: ¥54.0 billion (venue construction ¥24.0B + operating costs ¥30.0B projected)
  • April 2021 Basic Plan: ¥68.0 billion (venue ¥32.0B + operations ¥36.0B)
  • April 2025, 26th Board Meeting: ¥77.7 billion (venue ¥41.7B + operations ¥36.0B) — venue cost increased by ¥9.7B citing rising material and labor costs
  • December 2025, 32nd Board Meeting: ¥95.3 billion (venue ¥41.7B + operations ¥53.6B) — operating costs increased by ¥17.6B (¥36.0B → ¥53.6B, a 1.49x jump)

The cumulative increase from the conceptual phase is +¥41.3 billion, a 1.76x expansion.

The Association cites "inflation" and "concrete planning informed by the Osaka-Kansai Expo experience" as reasons. But note the timing of the December 2025 operating cost increase.

On March 18, 2025, in the Yokohama City Council Budget Special Committee, Yasuhiko Furuya (Japanese Communist Party, leader of the JCP group, Tsurumi-ku) questioned the venue construction cost increase from ¥32.0B to ¥41.7B, while also warning that "operating cost increases will be inevitable."

Nine months later, on December 17, 2025, the 32nd Board Meeting raised operating costs from ¥36.0B to ¥53.6B (+¥17.6B). Furuya's concerns were realized through the operating cost increase nine months later.

2The Confirmed ¥41.7 Billion in Funding

The ¥41.7 billion venue construction cost is structured under the June 22, 2021 cabinet understanding, with the national government, local governments, and private sector each bearing one-third:

  • National government: ¥13.9 billion (Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries + Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism)
  • Local government: ¥13.9 billion (Yokohama City + Kanagawa Prefecture)
  • Private donations: ¥13.9 billion

This is the same pattern used at the Osaka-Kansai Expo, the Aichi Expo, and the 1990 Osaka Flower Expo — a "standard rule" for hosting expos in Japan.

The ¥13.9 billion private portion will be collected from over 100 donor companies. Looking at the Association's published donor list (as of April 30, 2026), the lineup is revealing.

Super general contractors: Taisei Corporation, Takenaka Corporation.

Mid-tier and medium-sized general contractors: Toda Corporation, Seibu Construction, Fujita, Zenitaka Corporation, Tekken Corporation, Asanuma Corporation, Sato Kogyo, Honma Corporation, Fukuda Corporation, Aisawa Industries, Daihon-Gumi, Wakachiku Construction, Kitano Construction, Tobishima Corporation, Dai Nippon Construction, Mabuchi Construction, Inuyama Tatemono, Omata-Gumi, Shirai-Gumi, Katsumata, Shibahashi Shokai, Daisoh Kensetsu, Taiyo Construction, Aomi Construction, Showa Construction, Itogumi Construction, Nihon Kokudo Kaihatsu, Matsuo Komuten, Kawamoto Industries, Seibu-Gumi, Rinkai Nissan Construction, Sakakura, Oriental Shiraishi, Fudo Tetra.

Road construction: NIPPO, Maeda Road Construction, Kashima Road, Nihon Doro.

Industry associations: Japan Federation of Construction Contractors (Social Contribution Activities Council).

In other words, the very companies expected to bid for the remaining ¥38.2 billion in venue construction work have donated to the Association in advance.

3Concentration Structure in 121 Confirmed Contracts

We extracted 175 contracts published on the Association's contract information page as of June 4, 2026, and analyzed 121 with confirmed vendor identification.

The largest contract is ¥4.00 billion: "Admission Ticket Sales-Related Services Joint Venture" for "Admission Ticket Sales-Related Services Provision Contract." Second is ¥1.958 billion to Daiwa Lease Corporation, Yokohama Branch, for "Logistics and Disaster Stockpile Warehouse Lease."

The top 10 vendors by aggregate contract value:

  1. Admission Ticket Sales-Related Services JV: ¥4.000 billion (1 contract)
  2. Daiwa Lease Corporation, Yokohama Branch: ¥2.101 billion (2 contracts)
  3. Yamashita PMC: ¥780 million (6 contracts)
  4. Times JV: ¥590 million (1 contract)
  5. Dentsu: ¥573 million (3 contracts)
  6. Nippon Express, Yokohama Branch: ¥543 million (2 contracts)
  7. Hakuhodo: ¥529 million (2 contracts)
  8. JTB Communication Design: ¥524 million (6 contracts)
  9. TOPPAN Edge / TOPPAN JV: ¥490 million (1 contract)
  10. PREC Research Institute JV (FY2023): ¥446 million (1 contract)

By bidding method (aggregating the "category" column on the Association's contract information page as of June 4, 2026): 122 contracts (70% of 175) were Request-for-Proposal (RFP), 23 were open competitive bidding. Discretionary contracts on the surface are zero, but the RFP method effectively identifies a "best proposal" through a formal competitive process with often very few bidders.

For example, the upstream security contract "Security Implementation Plan Drafting" (November 19, 2025, ALSOK Corporation, ¥6.63 million) had only 2 bidders, with the other being "SPORTS Edge" — not even a security specialist. This becomes important in the context of the "3-year security cascade" below.

4Director Affiliates and Contract Awards

We examined which of the Association's 27 directors' affiliated companies or group entities have received contracts.

Direct conflicts of interest appear in 2 cases. Seibu Landscape JV — affiliated with Vice Chairman Shinya Wada (Chairman of the Japan Landscape Construction Association) — received ¥70 million and ¥50 million in June 2024 and November 2025 respectively (total ¥120 million).

But when affiliates and regular contractors are included, the picture changes substantially.

Director Keiko Tashiro serves as Vice Chair of the Japan Association of Corporate Executives, and as Director and Executive Vice President of Daiwa Securities Group Inc. Daiwa Lease Corporation Yokohama Branch — same Daiwa Group — has received over ¥2.1 billion in multiple contracts (Logistics warehouse lease ¥1.958B + temporary building leases).

Vice Chairman Ken Kobayashi (Chairman of the Japan Chamber of Commerce, former Mitsubishi Corporation Chairman), Director Toshikazu Miyanaga (Chairman of the Association's Finance Committee, former Mitsubishi Heavy Industries President), Director Mari Yoshitaka (Visiting Professor at University of Tokyo, MUFG-related) — with three Mitsubishi-related directors, Mitsubishi-group companies (Mitsubishi Research Institute, etc.) have received approximately ¥120 million across multiple contracts.

The principal breakdown of these concentrated awards: Daiwa Group ¥2.192B, TOPPAN/Toppan ¥988M, Dentsu ¥774M, Hakuhodo ¥667M, JTB ¥583M, Mitsubishi Group ¥120M, NHK-related ¥37M. Total: 40 contracts, ¥5.36 billion.

This means approximately 30% of the ¥17.5 billion total of the 121 confirmed contracts is concentrated among specific Keidanren-affiliated and large-event regular vendors.

To be clear: this concentration does not imply a causal "they received contracts because they are director-affiliated" relationship. The RFP method evaluates "best proposals," and expo operation experience is often considered, so concentration among major regular vendors can result naturally. Still, the fact that donor companies and director-affiliated group entities receive contracts through procurement (RFP method) where the evaluation process cannot be fully externally verified is a structure worth making visible.

5The ¥41.8 Billion Admission Fee Calculation

Of the ¥95.3 billion, the ¥53.6 billion operating costs are to be covered by ¥41.8 billion in admission fees and ¥11.8 billion in other revenue.

The premise for ¥41.8 billion admission revenue is 15 million visitors (10 million projected as paying customers).

The basis for this projection was questioned by Constitutional Democratic Party of Japan member Shuji Inadomi at the House of Representatives Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism Committee on March 9, 2022, during deliberation of the International Horticultural Expo Special Measures Act.

Inadomi cited Yokohama City's basic concept and pressed:

Looking through various materials, there don't seem to be specific grounds for how to attract these 10 million people. The only thing I found is the basic concept proposal for the Kamiseya International Horticultural Expo, which projected over 15 million visitors based on pre-COVID assumptions.

It states: the Osaka Flower Expo drew 23 million; other international horticultural expos drew 2-16 million; the 2005 Aichi Expo drew 22 million; the Yokohama Urban Greening Fair drew about 6 million; therefore, 15 million-plus visitors can be expected. But this seems somewhat too rough, doesn't it? Doesn't the foundation need to be more solid?

10 million visitors is a substantial number, so I'd like to ask about the basis.

Then-Minister of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism Tetsuo Saito (Komeito) responded:

The participant count for the 2027 International Horticultural Expo is a figure calculated by Yokohama City, referring to past expo performance and based on the catchment population for this expo.

Specifically, since past international expos and horticultural expos have drawn many visitors from the surrounding area, and visitor count is influenced by catchment population, the ratio of visitor count to catchment population is approximately 30-40 percent even for small international horticultural expos, and based on Yokohama City's current catchment population, the visitor count was calculated.

Yokohama City's catchment population = approximately 41.2 million in the Greater Tokyo Area. Three to four-tenths of that yields 15 million. That is the entire basis for the projection.

In other words, the national government did not perform independent verification. It simply adopted the figure Yokohama City had calculated as "30-40% of the Greater Tokyo population will attend."

There is no actual confirmation of attendance, no booking data, no analysis of access feasibility. The reasoning is: "Since 41.2 million people live in the Greater Tokyo area, 30-40% of them — 15 million — will come." This is a desk calculation.

6The Original New Transit System Plan Did Not Materialize

If 15 million visitors are projected, transit infrastructure to move them is a prerequisite. But that prerequisite was already abandoned at the time of the 2022 Special Measures Act deliberation.

Inadomi continued:

Regarding moving these 10 million visitors to the venue — there is no nearby rail; trains are about 2 km away. Without building some new transit system, you cannot transport 10 million people.

Yokohama City approached Yokohama Seaside Line Corp. (60% owned by Yokohama City as a third-sector corporation) for participation in building a new 2.2-km transit system from Seya Station to the venue. The approach was made in September 2021, and in November 2021, Seaside Line declined. I normally would have expected them to accept, but they declined. Yokohama City itself, in December 2021, gave up the 2.2-km rail construction in time for the Expo.

The reason, according to Seaside Line, is that the demand projections presented by Yokohama City — visitor estimates, modal split, etc. — could significantly vary, that the demand projections differed greatly in their understanding, that is, it would not be profitable.

The infrastructure operator closest to the venue (a third-sector corporation 60% owned by Yokohama City) declined participation in the new transit system, citing "different demand projection estimates" and "profitability concerns."

Minister Saito responded:

Regarding this new transit system, completion by the time of the Expo has become difficult, as I understand it. The International Horticultural Expo Association and Yokohama City are considering alternative transport methods centered on group buses by travel agencies and shuttle buses directly connecting railway stations to the venue.

The alternative is bus transit. But the station capacity projections shown by then-Director-General Yoshimasa Uno of the MLIT Urban Bureau were:

  • Seya Station: 31,261 people/day
  • Mitsukyō Station: 2,342 people/day
  • Minami-machida Grandberry Park Station: 1,721 people/day

Total approximately 35,000 people/day. Against a projected daily attendance of about 80,000 (15 million / 192 days of operation), station capacity falls considerably short. The remainder is to be moved by shuttle buses, with dedicated lanes and surrounding road widening also "under consideration."

The original new transit system was abandoned, and the transit plan was switched to shuttle bus-centered. Whether bus transit can realistically move 15 million visitors will be tested after opening.

7The Special Measures Act Passed Without Debate

The March 9, 2022 House of Representatives Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism Committee deliberation proceeded as a formal procedure for passing the Special Measures Act. Of the 45 statements, Constitutional Democratic Party members Shuji Inadomi and Makoto Yamazaki, and Yushi-no-kai member Nobuyuki Fukushima, sequentially pointed out the weak basis for visitor projections, the collapse of transit access, and the issue of overusing special legislation.

Fukushima's observation was particularly incisive:

If the postal arrangement has such benefits, then we could similarly create a special law to add donations to NTT phone fees, or add them to JR fares. It's not good to simply do it the same way as last time.

"Because it's the same pattern as last time (Osaka Expo), so this time too" had become the reason for creating the special law — Fukushima critiqued the structure of enacting individual special laws for each expo.

But near the end of deliberation, Committee Chair Kazuyuki Nakane (LDP) announced:

We move to discussion, but since there are no requests for discussion, we proceed directly to the vote. Those in favor of the motion will please rise.

The motion passed without any discussion request. The bill was approved. Opposition concerns were recorded in the proceedings, but did not result in amendments.

The following day, March 10, 2022, in the House Steering Committee, only the Japanese Communist Party and Reiwa Shinsengumi recorded opposition to the agenda's Special Measures Act-related items (per Committee Director Kenji Okada's procedural report).

8The Distorted Voting Structure in Local Government

The Special Measures Act has been advanced at the local government level with a non-bipartisan, highly skewed voting structure.

In the Yokohama City Council (86 members), on February 21, 2019, the "Yokohama City Council Members' Association to Make the International Horticultural Expo Hosting in Yokohama a Success" was established. It included 72 members: from LDP, Komeito, Mingiken Forum, Yokohama-kai, and independents. The chair was Ken Matsumoto (LDP, then-Speaker of the City Council).

This means 72 out of 86 members (83.7%) belong to a pro-hosting faction.

Opposition has come only from the Japanese Communist Party members:

  • First Regular Session 2018: Tamio Kawaji (JCP, Asahi-ku) made the opposition speech against Resolution 15 "Resolution Calling for the International Horticultural Expo Hosting in Yokohama"
  • November 2021: A formal proposal calling for "Review of the Excessive Yokohama Horticultural Expo Plan" and "Cancellation of the Kamiseya Line Plan"
  • March 18, 2025, Budget Special Committee: Yasuhiko Furuya opposed the venue construction cost increase from ¥32.0B to ¥41.7B and warned that operating cost increases would be inevitable (subsequently realized)

At the national Diet level, only the JCP and Reiwa Shinsengumi have expressed opposition.

The Constitutional Democratic Party of Japan has pursued the weak basis of projections through committee questioning but has ultimately voted for the legislation. The largest opposition party has not led the opposition.

The hosting was initiated by former Yokohama Mayor Fumiko Hayashi (2009-2021) and then-Chief Cabinet Secretary Yoshihide Suga (House of Representatives, Kanagawa District 2; later Prime Minister).

However, current Mayor Takeharu Yamanaka — elected with the support of CDPJ and JCP-aligned minority parties — takes a pro-Expo stance, making "support for hosting" the dominant position across both ruling and opposition parties.

9The Association's Financial Foundation — A Body Running on Borrowing and Unpaid Liabilities

The financial statements of the Japan Association for the International Horticultural Expo 2027 at fiscal year-end 2024 reveal the body's thin operational base:

  • Total assets: ¥7.13 billion
  • Net assets: ¥110 million
  • Short-term borrowing from Yokohama Bank: ¥4.27 billion
  • Unpaid liabilities: ¥2.72 billion
  • Construction in progress (venue design): ¥3.14 billion
DATA: Japan Association for the International Horticultural Expo 2027 — FY2024 Balance Sheet
DATA: Association financial statements (FY2024). Designated Net Assets = National + Local subsidies + Designated donations / General Net Assets = Discretionary balance

An Association with only ¥110 million in net assets is operating with ¥4.27 billion in bank borrowing and ¥2.72 billion in unpaid liabilities while advancing venue design. The full national funding (¥13.9B) and local government support (¥13.9B) are still to be deployed.

FY2024 subsidies received: ¥1.05B national + ¥1.05B local = ¥2.1B total. Relative to the ¥95.3 billion project scale, this is still only the opening chapter.

Inside the ¥114 Million Net Assets — An Entity Sustained by Subsidies

But this surface figure of "¥114 million net assets" hides the actual structure. Breaking down the financial statements reveals the composition:

  • Designated net assets: ¥3.085 billion (positive) — Funds received from national/local governments and corporate donors with designated use ("for venue construction"). Composition: National subsidies ¥1.047B + Local government subsidies ¥1.047B + Designated donations ¥991M
  • General net assets: ▲¥2.971 billion (negative) — The portion the Association can freely use. The deficit has been expanding for three consecutive years

Laying out the three-year transition makes the structure clear:

Fiscal Year-EndDesignated Net AssetsGeneral Net AssetsTotal Net Assets
2022+¥116M▲¥1.038B▲¥922M
2023+¥965M▲¥2.101B▲¥1.136B
2024+¥3.085B▲¥2.971B+¥114M

While designated net assets surged from ¥116M to ¥3.085B, general net assets expanded the deficit from ▲¥1.038B to ▲¥2.971B at an annual pace of ¥800M-1B. The Association generates no income on its own — it is an entity sustained by subsidies.

Actual business revenue (admission, merchandise, etc.) only begins after the Expo opens in March 2027. Until then, the general net asset deficit will continue to be filled by subsidies and borrowing.

See the visualized transition chart in Dashboard Section 04 "Association Balance Sheet".

If admission revenue of ¥41.8 billion falls short of projections, the compensation mechanism is not specified in the act or the board materials. Article 11 of the act stipulates "subsidies may be provided within the budget" — this provides a legal basis for additional public funding. Whether tax money actually fills the gap depends on the policy decisions of the national and local governments at that time.

10The Structure of "Spending Other People's Money on Other People"

Summarizing the structure observed:

  • Project cost grew from ¥54.0B (2018 concept) to ¥95.3B, a 1.76x increase
  • Of confirmed ¥41.7B: tax-funded portion is ¥13.9B national + ¥13.9B local = ¥27.8B
  • The remaining ¥13.9B is contributed by 100+ donor companies (including 50+ general contractors)
  • These contractors are also the candidates for bidding on the upcoming ¥38.2B venue construction work
  • Of 121 confirmed contracts, 40 contracts totaling ¥5.36B (approximately 30%) are concentrated among director-affiliated, Keidanren-affiliated, and large-event regular vendors
  • The remaining ¥53.6B depends on the projected ¥41.8B in admission revenue
  • The basis for this admission revenue is the desk calculation "30-40% of the Greater Tokyo population will attend"
  • The original new transit system was abandoned; the transit plan was switched to shuttle bus-centered
  • The Special Measures Act passed without debate
  • 72 of 86 Yokohama City Council members are pro-hosting
  • Only the JCP and Reiwa Shinsengumi expressed opposition at the national level

Economist Milton Friedman classified spending into four categories:

  1. Your own money, on yourself → Most careful (mindful of both cost and quality)
  2. Your own money, on someone else → Cost-conscious
  3. Someone else's money, on yourself → Quality-conscious but not cost-conscious
  4. Someone else's money, on someone else → Neither is closely monitored

Public spending falls into the fourth category. This is why, when asked in the Diet "what is the basis for 15 million visitors?", the answer "30-40% of the Greater Tokyo population" can suffice to end the discussion.

If Minister Tetsuo Saito himself were to pay ¥13.9 billion out of his personal savings, he would certainly never approve based on such rough verification. People verify repeatedly when buying a house or a car. But once it becomes tax money, verification becomes lax.

This is the structure of "other people's money becomes carelessly spent."

11Citizens Are Also Part of the Structure

There is one more layer to consider.

Why can't the structure of "spending other people's money on other people" be prevented? Because citizens are not choosing candidates who seriously verify the use of tax money when voting.

The media, too, tend to report only "the Expo will be fun" and "the economic ripple effect is trillions of yen," without exploring this structural verification.

The Yokohama Flower Expo may indeed be an enjoyable event. There is value in a flower and greenery-themed exhibition in itself. But as those paying the cost, citizens must ask:

  • Is the 15 million visitor projection truly justified?
  • Is ¥95.3 billion an appropriate project scale?
  • If the ¥41.8 billion admission revenue falls short, who bears responsibility?
  • Why a one-time event rather than distributed investment in elderly care, healthcare, pensions, or aging infrastructure?
  • Is the structure of 100+ general contractors donating in advance truly for "public benefit"?

Without asking these questions before heading to the polls, the next expo will proceed in the same structure.

12Conclusion

On March 19, 2027, the gates will open.

Whether admission revenue of ¥41.8 billion will be reached, no one yet knows.

If it falls short, Article 11 of the Special Measures Act ("subsidies may be provided within the budget") provides a legal basis for additional public funding. Whether tax money actually fills the gap depends on the policy decisions of the government and local authorities at that time.

Do we wait until it happens, or question it before it happens?
