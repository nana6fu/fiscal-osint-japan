Tracing the ¥80 Trillion US Investment Pledge Through Japan's FILP Special Account

JBIC capital injections from Japan's FILP Special Account expanded 4.2× from FY2023 to FY2026. Japan's Ministry of Finance itself labels its FY2025 supplementary budget as "responding to US tariffs." We trace the structural pathway through 14 primary sources from both Japanese and US governments.

📅 Published: 2026/05/22 🔄 Updated: 2026/05/23 $550B / ¥80 trillion FILP Special Account JBIC OSINT 📝 ~3,500 words / ~20 min read

① The $550 billion (~¥80 trillion) US investment pledge announced in July 2025 is not a "painted rice cake" (an empty promise). It is already in motion through a concrete fiscal pathway: loans and equity from Japan's Fiscal Investment and Loan Program (FILP) Special Account directed to the Japan Bank for International Cooperation (JBIC).
② JBIC inflows expanded from FY2023 ¥1.0557 trillion to FY2026 ¥4.4177 trillion — a 4.2× increase from FY2023 to FY2026. The Ministry of Finance itself labels Japan's FY2025 first supplementary budget as "responding to US tariffs."
③ However, Japanese and US explanations of the same agreement reveal clear discrepancies — from the very nature of the deal (US calls it "investment," Japan calls it "a credit facility framework") to profit distribution, the existence of joint documents, and the status of the 21-company list.

1. Introduction — Not an Empty Promise

On July 22, 2025, US President Donald Trump announced on his social network Truth Social that Japan had agreed to invest $550 billion (approximately ¥80 trillion at then-prevailing exchange rates of ¥145/$) in the United States. Japanese media reported it as "US-bound investment" or a "massive investment commitment," but the actual substance remained ambiguous as months passed.

This article cross-references 14 primary sources from both Japanese and US governments — including documents from the Ministry of Finance, Cabinet Secretariat, Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry, Prime Minister's Office, the White House (executive order, three fact sheets, and the memorandum of understanding) — to trace which fiscal pocket the "¥80 trillion" actually comes from, by what pathway, and to what destination.

The conclusion, stated upfront: the core of the ¥80 trillion pledge has already begun moving as equity investments and loans from Japan's Fiscal Investment and Loan Program (FILP) Special Account to the Japan Bank for International Cooperation (JBIC). The scale, verifiable from the Ministry of Finance's official settlement and budget documents, has expanded approximately 4.2× over the four fiscal years from FY2023 (R5) to FY2026 (R8).

Note for international readers: Japan's Special Accounts (特別会計) are not equivalent to "special funds" in other jurisdictions. They are separate fiscal pockets — 19 in total across the central government — that operate alongside the general account. Unlike the general account, which resets annually under the single-year budget principle (unspent funds in principle expire at fiscal year-end), Special Accounts maintain fund balances that roll over year to year, accumulating into trillion-yen-scale reserves. This rollover structure becomes central to understanding the ¥80 trillion pledge in Section 7.

2. Background: What is the $550 Billion Framework?

The agreement began with President Trump's July 22, 2025 announcement on Truth Social:

🇺🇸 US / President Trump

"We just completed a massive Deal with Japan, perhaps the largest Deal ever made. Japan will invest, at my direction, $550 Billion Dollars into the United States, which will receive 90% of the Profits. This Deal will create Hundreds of Thousands of Jobs — There has never been anything like it."

— Source: Truth Social (July 22, 2025), quoted verbatim by Newsweek, Al Jazeera, and others.

The following day, July 23, 2025, the White House released an official fact sheet:

🇺🇸 US / White House Fact Sheet

"Japan will invest $550 billion directed by the United States to rebuild and expand core American industries. ... At President Trump's direction, these funds will be targeted toward the revitalization of America's strategic industrial base. ... The United States will retain 90% of the profits from this investment."

— Source: White House Fact Sheet (July 23, 2025)

The Japanese side's explanation that same day struck a very different tone. In the afternoon, then-Prime Minister Shigeru Ishiba stated:

🇯🇵 Japan / Prime Minister Ishiba

"To accurately characterize what was agreed: government-affiliated financial institutions will be enabled to provide up to $550 billion in equity investments, loans, and loan guarantees for US-bound investments by Japanese companies in critical sectors such as pharmaceuticals and semiconductors. This investment is to be implemented through equity investments and loans from the Japan Bank for International Cooperation (JBIC) and guarantees from Nippon Export and Investment Insurance (NEXI)."

— Source: Prime Minister's Office, Press conference on the Japan-US tariff agreement (July 23, 2025)

"Japan will invest $550 billion" (US side) versus "Government-affiliated financial institutions will be enabled to provide investments, loans, and loan guarantees" (Japan side). These two sides are supposedly describing the same agreement — yet the very nature of the deal disagrees: is Japan investing, or are financial institutions creating a support facility?

3. Five Discrepancies in Japan-US Understanding

Ten months after the agreement (as of May 2026), multiple discrepancies can be confirmed from primary sources. We organize them below.

3-1. The Nature of the Deal: "Investment" or "Credit Facility Framework"?

Trump went further in his July 24, 2025 remarks:

🇺🇸 US / President Trump (July 24, 2025)

"Japan is putting up $550 billion in order to lower their tariffs a little bit. They put up, as you could call it, seed money. Let's call it seed money."

"It's not a loan or anything, it's a signing bonus."

— Source: AP via MarketBeat (July 24, 2025)

"Not a loan or anything — a signing bonus." This is clearly a different characterization than the Japanese side's "framework for equity investments, loans, and loan guarantees."

Japan's Cabinet Secretariat document (dated July 25, 2025) is consistent with Ishiba's explanation:

🇯🇵 Japan / Cabinet Secretariat

"To realize this, government-affiliated financial institutions will be enabled to provide up to $550 billion in equity investments, loans, and loan guarantees."

— Source: Cabinet Secretariat, Japan-US Tariff Negotiations: Outline of the Japan-US Agreement (July 25, 2025)

3-2. No Joint Document — A Foreign Ministry Spokesman Admits

For weeks after the July 22 announcement, no formal joint document existed. The Ministry of Foreign Affairs itself acknowledged this:

🇯🇵 Japan / Foreign Ministry Spokesman

"As you know, ultimately an agreement serving the national interests of both countries was reached, based on the July 22 exchange between Minister for Economic Revitalization Akazawa and President Trump. Given the negotiations went down to the wire, no joint document was prepared."

— Source: Ministry of Foreign Affairs, Press Briefing by Foreign Ministry Spokesperson Kitamura (July 30, 2025)

In other words, from Trump's "Japan will invest, at my direction, $550 Billion" tweet until the MOU signing on September 4, 2025 — a span of roughly six weeks — neither country had a shared document confirming what had been agreed. ⚠️ Note AP reported during this same period that it had interviewed Japan's Cabinet Office and received the following explanation:

🇯🇵 Japan / Cabinet Office (via AP interview)

"Japan's Cabinet Office indicated that the investment involves a credit facility from state-affiliated financial institutions, and further terms are still being worked out."

"Japanese trade negotiators have highlighted that no legally binding agreement is in place and believe that differences in understanding may need to be clarified."

— Source: AP via MarketBeat (July 2025)

That Japan's own government publicly acknowledged "differences in understanding may need to be clarified" demonstrates the agreement's instability.

3-3. September 4 MOU Signing — Japan Released It, the US Did Not

On September 4, 2025, in Washington D.C., then-Minister for Economic Revitalization Akazawa Ryosei and US Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick signed the "Memorandum of Understanding on Strategic Investment Between the Government of Japan and the Government of the United States of America." Japan released a provisional Japanese translation on the Cabinet Secretariat's website:

🇯🇵 Japan / MOU Provisional Translation

"Recognizing that it is in the best interest of both nations to promote economic and national security interests, Japan agrees that $550 billion will be invested in the United States (capital commitment) across various sectors including but not limited to semiconductors, pharmaceuticals, metals, critical minerals, shipbuilding, energy (including pipelines), and artificial intelligence/quantum computing."

Paragraph 7: "Japan shall, on or after a date 45 business days following the date on which Japan is notified that the President has selected an investment, contribute immediately available US dollar-denominated funds to the account(s) designated by the US Investment Accelerator."

Paragraph 8: "If Japan elects not to provide funding, the United States may impose a tariff rate or rates on imports from Japan to the United States at a rate determined by the President."

— Source: Cabinet Secretariat, MOU on Strategic Investment (Provisional Translation) (signed September 4, 2025)

The MOU's provisional translation uses terms like "capital commitment" and "immediately available US dollar-denominated funds." This carries a different nuance than PM Ishiba's earlier "enabling financial institutions to provide investments, loans, and loan guarantees." The MOU language tracks closer to the US side's "investment commitment."

⚠️ Note "Capital commitment" in standard finance refers to a binding obligation by limited partners to fund a private equity vehicle. The MOU's use of this term is unusual: Paragraph 21 of the same document explicitly states it has no legal binding force, yet uses language typically reserved for binding obligations. This linguistic mismatch is part of the structural ambiguity tracked throughout this article.

And the US side has not released the same MOU. The US independent research organization Just Security pointed this out:

🇺🇸 US / Just Security

"The White House's executive order only referenced the $550 billion investment without explaining how the deal will operate. And while Japan has publicly released the memorandum, the Trump administration has not — another omission."

— Source: Just Security (November 2025)

Some US analysts have criticized the deal as operating like a "$550 billion slush fund" outside the congressional appropriations process.

3-4. Multiple Versions of the Profit-Sharing Explanation

The "10% Japan / 90% US" profit split is frequently cited in media reports. But the explanation has shifted depending on timing and document.

Date / Source Stated profit distribution
Cabinet Secretariat document, July 25, 2025 "Determined based on the contributions and risks borne by each side"
Akazawa on NHK, July 26, 2025 "Japan initially proposed 50:50 → final agreement is Japan 10% : US 90%"
Trump on Truth Social, July 22, 2025 "90% of the Profits"
MOU Provisional Translation, Paragraph 13 (Sept 4 signing) (a) 50:50 up to the Deemed Allocation Amount, (b) thereafter US 90 : Japan 10 (two-tier structure)

At the time of MOU signing, the two-tier structure was made explicit: 50:50 up to the "Deemed Allocation Amount," then 90:10 beyond that. ⚠️ Note The "Deemed Allocation Amount" is complexly defined in the MOU's appendix: it equals "the amount invested multiplied by a deemed interest rate" + "divided by the expected project duration" + "plus carryforward amount." Effectively, this can be read as a design where the first tier (50:50) corresponds to recovery of principal-equivalent, and the second tier (90:10) applies to excess returns thereafter (inference involved).

3-5. The 21-Company List: Japan Calls Them "Candidates," US Says "Commitments Being Fulfilled"

On October 28, 2025, coinciding with Trump's visit to Japan, both governments released a "Japan-US Joint Fact Sheet on Investment" disclosing 21 companies (22 firms) and project amounts. But how these companies are characterized differs between the two sides:

🇺🇸 US / White House Fact Sheet

"Today in Tokyo, President Donald J. Trump announced major projects advancing Japan's previous $550 billion investment commitment to the United States. ... Japan and various Japanese companies have outlined their commitments to pursuing the following investments."

— Source: White House Fact Sheet (October 28, 2025)

Meanwhile, METI Minister Akazawa Ryosei made the following statement at his post-Cabinet press conference the same day:

🇯🇵 Japan / METI Minister Akazawa

"Whether these will actually become part of the initial $550 billion is something that's determined through the MOU's procedures, through the Consultation Committee and Investment Committee. So in that sense, they currently have nothing to do with the MOU process. They are merely candidates, or 'seeds,' that both Japanese and US governments will work together to bring into the process."

— Source: METI, Minister Akazawa's Post-Cabinet Press Conference (October 28, 2025)

The US side describes these as "fulfillment of commitments," while the Japan side describes them as "candidates or seeds, with no relation yet to the MOU process." That both governments released the same 21-company list with such different tones again highlights the gap in understanding.

Japan's own version of the joint fact sheet (released jointly by METI and the Ministry of Finance) uses yet different language:

🇯🇵 Japan / Joint Fact Sheet (Provisional Translation)

"On the occasion of President Trump's visit to Japan, both governments welcomed that companies of both countries have expressed interest in forming projects in the following areas."

"Both governments expressed strong expectations that various business initiatives — including those that may fall within the scope of the September 4 MOU on $550 billion strategic investment — will be advanced to strengthen Japan-US supply chain resilience."

— Source: Ministry of Finance & METI, Japan-US Joint Fact Sheet on Investment (Provisional Translation) (October 28, 2025)

"Companies have expressed interest" / "Strong expectations expressed" — Japan's fact sheet positions this as government supporting corporate decision-making. Meanwhile, the US fact sheet writes "Japan committed" and "various Japanese companies have outlined their commitments." The same joint fact sheet (supposedly) — but the commitment intensity differs significantly.

4. Ministry of Finance Explicitly Labels FY2025 Supplementary Budget as "Responding to US Tariffs"

The substance of the agreement cannot be grasped from documents alone. We need to trace which pocket actual funds moved from, and to where. Japan's Ministry of Finance budget documents are eloquent on this point.

Japan's FY2025 first supplementary budget (R7補正特第1号) was approved by Cabinet on November 28, 2025 and enacted on December 16, 2025. The Investment Account of the FILP Special Account's supplementary budget explanation reads:

🇯🇵 Japan / Ministry of Finance, FY2025 First Supplementary Budget

"As part of the 'Comprehensive Economic Measures to Realize a Strong Economy,' this budget adds equity investment and other funds to strengthen the financial base of the Japan Bank for International Cooperation's Japan Strategic Investment Facility, which is undertaken to respond to US tariffs for supporting Japanese companies."

— Source: Ministry of Finance, FY2025 Budget (FILP Special Account, First Supplementary)

The Ministry of Finance itself explicitly states in its budget documents: "responding to US tariffs" and "JBIC Japan Strategic Investment Facility". This is the first concrete trace of the ¥80 trillion US investment commitment being incorporated into Japan's formal budget process.

This supplementary budget added ¥270 billion in JBIC equity. The next table shows the five-year trend.

Three notable features emerge:

① FY2023 → FY2024: a temporary decline. JBIC inflows decreased from ¥1,055.7B to ¥275.0B — to about a quarter. This is the normal operating pace before the ¥80 trillion agreement.

② FY2025 initial budget: only modest growth. At the time of the FY2025 initial budget (enacted March 2025), the ¥80 trillion agreement (July 2025) did not yet exist. Inflows were ¥820.0B — increased from FY2024 but within "normal operations."

③ FY2025 supplementary budget: sharp acceleration; FY2026 expanded further. Following the ¥80 trillion agreement, the FY2025 first supplementary budget (enacted December 2025) expanded JBIC loans from ¥720.0B to ¥2,660.0B — a 3.7× jump. The subsequent FY2026 initial budget (enacted March 2026) further raised loans to ¥4,252.7B. Total JBIC inflows expanded 4.18× from FY2023 to FY2026.

These figures are independently verifiable from official Ministry of Finance budget documents, and demonstrate that capital injections into JBIC accelerated sharply, timed with the ¥80 trillion agreement.

Minister Akazawa explained the funding source as follows:

🇯🇵 Japan / Minister Akazawa (September 12, 2025)

"Regarding JBIC's loans, I believe about half can be covered by investment income from the Foreign Exchange Fund Special Account (FEFSA), etc. There is no need to sell US Treasury securities. The rest will be addressed through government-guaranteed dollar-denominated bond issuances and currency swaps."

— Source: Nikkei (September 12, 2025) / from his appearance on ReHacQ program

The FEFSA's fund balance at the end of FY2024 (as of March 31, 2025) was approximately ¥187 trillion, the majority held as foreign currency assets including US Treasury securities. ⚠️ Inference Based on Akazawa's explanation, the funds JBIC will lend to US companies in dollars will partly come from investment income (such as interest on US Treasury securities) earned by the FEFSA. The structure is: using interest from US Treasuries to lend to US companies (this involves interpretation/inference of Akazawa's statement).

6. Investment Decision Structure and the 21 Companies

MOU paragraphs 1–5 define the governance of investment decisions:

🇯🇵 Japan / MOU Provisional Translation

Paragraph 1: "The US President will select investments from among those recommended by the Investment Committee."

Paragraph 2: "Investments will be made from time to time during the period from the date of this MOU to January 19, 2029."

Paragraph 4: "The Investment Committee will be chaired by the US Secretary of Commerce, and will include other members selected from time to time by the Investment Committee."

Paragraph 11: "The United States will establish a new Investment SPV in connection with each investment. Each Investment SPV will be managed and governed by the United States or its designee acting as the general partner."

— Source: Cabinet Secretariat, MOU on Strategic Investment (Provisional Translation)

Three important structural features emerge. First, investment-selection authority lies with the US President. The Investment Committee's chair is the US Secretary of Commerce (Lutnick). Japan can provide input through its Consultation Committee, but the final decision rests with the US president. Second, investments run until January 19, 2029 — the end of Trump's term. This is the source of the "¥80 trillion in 3.5 years" timeline. Third, management of the Investment SPVs is also on the US side. Japan provides the capital; the US handles governance.

7. Structural Issue — The Nature of Special Accounts as a Container

Japan's FILP Special Account has a fundamentally different character from the general account. That difference is: it accumulates year over year.

Japan's general account operates on a single-year budget principle — in principle, unspent funds expire at year-end (carryover exists but is exceptional). The FILP Special Account, by contrast, maintains a fund balance (stock) across fiscal years. As of end-FY2024, the Fiscal Loan Fund Account's fund balance was ¥126.49 trillion. From this pool, loans to JBIC and others are executed each year, and repayments are reinvested.

JBIC capital injections accumulate on JBIC's balance sheet as "outstanding overseas loans." When JBIC then provides funds to US companies as a US-bound investment facility, this appears on the US company side as "government-guaranteed loans." ⚠️ Interpretation The ¥80 trillion US-bound investment, in other words, is designed as a structure where capital injections into JBIC continue year by year within the FILP Special Account container, accumulating over the 3.5-year period until the end of Trump's term (January 19, 2029). This is the fiscal meaning of MOU Paragraph 2: "Investments will be made from time to time... until January 19, 2029."

The relationship with the congressional appropriations process is also being questioned. On the US side, Just Security points out:

🇺🇸 US / Just Security

"The deal, formalized in a memorandum of understanding, operates outside the congressional appropriations process. ... [The] $550 billion slush fund would amount to eight percent of federal spending in FY2024, or more than the GDP of 34 states and all but 26 countries."

— Source: Just Security (November 13, 2025)

That an independent US research organization is characterizing this as "a slush fund operating outside congressional appropriations" is significant.

US media Axios also raised opacity concerns early on:

🇺🇸 US / Axios

"It's unclear what legal entities — on either the Japanese or U.S. side — would be involved, or what would be in it for Japanese companies if they have neither control over the investments nor a financial return."

— Source: Axios (July 24, 2025)

The question of what tangible benefit Japanese companies receive has been raised in US media from the outset.

On the Japan side, Kiuchi Takahide, an economist at Nomura Research Institute, offers this analysis:

🇯🇵 Japan / Kiuchi Takahide, Nomura Research Institute

"The substance is close to what the Trump administration had been explaining all along — strongly characterized as US-led and US-advantaged. One could say it has become quite an unequal arrangement for Japan."

"This is a scheme convenient for Japan to provide funds to the US. The fact that Japan's government-affiliated financial institutions — which originally exist for the growth and development of Japanese companies and the benefit of Japanese citizens — have become a scheme providing funds for the US economy and industry is a major issue."

— Source: Nomura Research Institute, Kiuchi Takahide's Economic Currents (March 10, 2026)

Independent analysts on both sides have raised structural concerns from their respective viewpoints. That is the objective picture at this moment.

8. Remaining Questions

Several questions about the ¥80 trillion US-bound investment cannot yet be answered from publicly available information.

① Progress of the 21 companies. The actual investment execution status of the 21 companies disclosed October 28, 2025 must be tracked individually through each company's securities reports and timely disclosures. In February 2026, the first three projects (gas thermal power, synthetic diamond, port improvement; total $36 billion) were announced, but updates since have been limited.

② The precise calculation of "Deemed Allocation Amount." The MOU Paragraph 13 profit-distribution boundary (50:50 → 90:10 two-tier structure) hinges on the "Deemed Allocation Amount," defined complexly in the MOU appendix. How this calculation actually plays out will vary by investment. Transparent disclosure is needed.

③ Risk burden in case of investment failure. MOU Paragraph 17 includes an exemption clause: "The United States, Japan, the Investment Committee, etc. shall not be liable for decisions made regarding investments." But if losses occur, on whose risk are the funds contributed by Japan held? This is not clear.

④ Legal binding and tariff retaliation. MOU Paragraph 21 explicitly states "no legal binding force," but Paragraph 8 stipulates "the US may impose tariffs if Japan does not provide funding." How should the de facto coercive force be evaluated?

⑤ Future plans for FEFSA investment income. Will Akazawa's statement that "half of JBIC's loans can be covered by FEFSA investment income" become the de facto scheme? Under Japan's current FEFSA disclosure framework, further transparency about how investment income is used will be necessary.

This article presents only the structural overview. Separate follow-up articles will address each of these questions.
