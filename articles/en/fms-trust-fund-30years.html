1. Introduction — The "Where Does the Money Actually Go" Question

The previous two articles in this series — "Tracing the ¥80 Trillion US Investment Pledge Through Japan's FILP Special Account" (Article #1) and "Japan's One-Time Defense Funding Scheme — The Special Law That Extracted ¥1.84 Trillion from Special Accounts, and the Tomahawks Bought at Twice the US Navy Price" (Article #2) — both addressed public capital that is about to flow, or is currently flowing, from Japan to the United States. The routes and legal foundations can be verified through primary sources: from the FILP Special Account through JBIC to U.S. companies; from the Foreign Exchange Fund and FILP Special Accounts through the general account to FMS and the U.S. Treasury.

This article asks a different question: what happens to money that has already been transferred? Under FMS (Foreign Military Sales), weapon deliveries are often delayed by several to over ten years. Japan transfers the full estimated procurement price in advance. During the intervening period, where is the money, and what is being done with it? Are the weapons actually being delivered? For payments not yet matched to delivered weapons, who is treating that money as theirs?

Here is the conclusion up front. On the Japanese side, the Board of Audit has been flagging the same problem continuously since FY1997 (Heisei 9) — for roughly 30 years. There are words of improvement, but the structure does not change. "Unresolved settlement amounts are at high levels," "Final settlement statements take considerable time to arrive," "Japan has not been requesting the return of refundable funds." This article reconstructs that structure using only primary sources.

Context for international readers: FMS is a U.S. system through which eligible foreign governments may purchase defense articles, services, and training from the U.S. Government, authorized by the Arms Export Control Act. Japan has been an FMS purchaser since 1954, when both countries signed the U.S.-Japan Mutual Defense Assistance (MDA) Agreement. As Japan's defense spending expands sharply under the FY2023–FY2027 Defense Buildup Program, FMS procurement has become a central conduit of U.S.-bound defense expenditure. Japan's Board of Audit — equivalent to the U.S. GAO — has independent audit authority and reports directly to the Diet (Parliament).

2. The Structure of the FMS Trust Fund — Weapon Payments Flow to Three Places

FMS, authorized by the U.S. Arms Export Control Act, is a system under which the U.S. Government provides defense articles, services, and training to foreign governments for a fee. The official framing is "government-to-government, not commercial transaction," and the principle is that "the U.S. Government does not profit from the transaction." However, as we will see, the operational structure of this principle has its own particularities.

In Japanese FMS procurement, payment is not made after the defense article or service is delivered. Instead, the entire "estimated procurement price" is paid in advance at the time of contract signing. The remitted dollars are pooled in an "interest-bearing account" inside the Federal Reserve Bank of New York. Japan's Board of Audit's FY2018 Diet-requested investigation report describes what happens next:

🇯🇵 Board of Audit FY2018 Diet-Requested Report (published October 18, 2019)

"The Trust Fund is an account into which the U.S. Government deposits, through a prescribed procedure from the interest-bearing account at the Federal Reserve Bank, the portion necessary for acquiring defense articles or services for the purchasing country — also called the 'holding account.'"

"The funds in the interest-bearing account that are not deposited into the Trust Fund are invested in U.S. Treasury securities and similar instruments at the Federal Reserve Bank, and the interest generated is managed in an account called '0QQ.'"

— Source: Japan's Board of Audit, FY2018 Diet-Requested Report, Chapter 2 "The FMS Mechanism"
🔗 📍 View original (Japanese)

In other words, the dollars Japan transfers as prepayment serve a dual role: weapon manufacturing funds, and U.S. Treasury securities investment funds. The longer weapon delivery and settlement are delayed, the longer the U.S. Treasury retains Japanese money to hold U.S. government debt.

A Private-Sector Analogy — Buying a $100 Million Custom Machine

To understand this structure intuitively, consider a private-sector analogy: you place an order with a U.S. vendor for a custom machine listed at $100 million. With administrative surcharges (general administration 3.2% + contract administration 1.2%), the total comes to roughly $104.4 million. You wire the full $104.4 million up front to the vendor's bank account at the time of contract signing.

💡 Private-Sector Analogy: FMS vs. Standard Commercial Practice

Stage FMS (Actual Practice) Private-Sector Equivalent
Contract Estimated $100M + G&A 3.2% + Contract Mgmt 1.2% ≈ $104.4M "I'll buy your custom machine for $104.4M including handling fees."
Payment Full amount wired to NY Fed interest-bearing account up front Full $104.4M wired in cash to vendor's account
Operation Only ~2 months of avg. payment goes to Trust Fund; remainder invested in U.S. Treasury securities Vendor orders only $5M worth of parts initially; parks the remaining $99M elsewhere and keeps the interest
Delivery Several to over ten years of delay "Your machine will be delivered in 5 years. We need to wait for other customers' orders to finish too."
Settlement Wait for final settlement statement; refunds require Japan's active request "We won't automatically refund any overpayment. Please send us a written claim."
Interest Accumulates in 0QQ account on U.S. side; not returned unless requested Investment interest also "ours unless you ask for it back"

No one would agree to these terms in a normal commercial transaction. FMS is presented as government-to-government with "the U.S. Government does not profit" — but its cash-management structure is functionally indistinguishable from commercial financing. That reading is not the editorial's invention; it is what Japan's Board of Audit makes evident through its primary-source documentation.

⚠️ Note This analogy is for intuitive understanding and not every contract operates exactly this way. Administrative surcharges include legitimate cost-recovery components, and the Trust Fund deposit ratio and interest treatment vary by contract and year. Individual cases are examined in subsequent chapters.

3. FY1997 — The Year Japan Joined ACCP, and the Year the Board of Audit First Raised the Alarm

Japan has been an FMS purchaser since the 1954 U.S.-Japan Mutual Defense Assistance (MDA) Agreement. For over 40 years, the unresolved-settlements problem did not surface publicly. The turning point came in FY1997 (Heisei 9).

That year, the United States had introduced the "Accelerated Case Closure Procedures (ACCP)" — a system targeting final settlement statement issuance within 2 years of delivery completion. Japan joined ACCP the same year. Simultaneously, Japan's Board of Audit, for the first time, addressed FMS unresolved-settlement problems in its FY1997 settlement audit report as a "matter requiring special note."

🇯🇵 Board of Audit FY2018 Diet-Requested Report, Chapter 3 "Audit Findings and Opinions"

"In its FY1997 settlement audit report, the Board of Audit included as a 'matter requiring special note' the situation in which, regarding FMS central procurement by the (then) Japan Defense Agency, unresolved settlement amounts had reached high levels due to non-delivery of procured items, and prepayments had become excessive due to overestimated quotations."

"In its FY2002 settlement audit report, the Board noted that despite the Ministry of Defense's efforts to improve FMS procurement, unresolved settlement amounts remained at high levels, and again addressed this as a 'matter requiring special note.'"

— Source: Board of Audit FY2018 Diet-Requested Report, Chapter 3
🔗 📍 View original (Japanese)

Subsequent years — FY2013, FY2016, and FY2018 (the comprehensive Diet-requested investigation) — saw the Board of Audit continue to flag the same fundamental problem. Reform efforts have been undertaken, but the absolute level of unresolved settlements has not declined.

The year Japan joined ACCP (1997) and the year the Board of Audit first flagged the unresolved-settlement problem (1997) are the same year. This is not coincidence; it suggests that operating ACCP made the unresolved-settlement structure visible.

For five consecutive years, unresolved settlements stayed in the ¥90–122 billion range annually. Over the 5-year period, approximately ¥542.4 billion in FMS prepayments accumulated as either undelivered items or items without completed settlement statements on the U.S. side. This exceeds the scale of "temporary administrative delays" and is more naturally read as structural accumulation.

The Board of Audit itself wrote in its findings:

🇯🇵 Board of Audit Findings

"Even taking into account the characteristics of FMS procurement, the number of unresolved cases and the unresolved settlement amounts remain at high levels."

"Despite the adoption of the Accelerated Case Closure Procedures, many cases had not received final settlement statements within 2 years after delivery completion."

"Settlement within 2 years" — the target that ACCP was designed to achieve — is not, in reality, being delivered. That is the Board of Audit's bottom line.

5. The F-35A Price Puzzle — Same Aircraft, $40–56M Per Unit Difference

Alongside the unresolved-settlement problem, the Board of Audit has flagged price transparency. The F-35A fighter jet provides a documented example of identical equipment being procured at different prices between Japan and the United States.

🇯🇵 Board of Audit FY2018 Diet-Requested Report, Chapter 2

"For F-35A Lot 9, the difference between Japan's price and the U.S. DoD's published price was $56.1 million per aircraft."

"For F-35A Lot 10 and later, the difference between Japan's price and the U.S. DoD's published price was approximately $40 million per aircraft."

— Source: Board of Audit FY2018 Diet-Requested Report, Figure 2-2-8 area
🔗 📍 View Chapter 2 (Japanese)

At JPY 157/USD (May 2026 reference), Lot 9 was about ¥8.8 billion per aircraft in difference, and Lot 10 and later about ¥6.3 billion per aircraft. Japan was procuring identical F-35As at $40–56 million more per unit than the U.S. DoD's own published procurement price.

The U.S. DSCA (Defense Security Cooperation Agency) explains this price differential through "specification differences (Japan-specific additional equipment)," "development cost-sharing (FACO / Final Assembly and Check-Out facility costs)," and "exchange-rate variation." The Board of Audit accepts parts of these explanations as legitimate. But it also writes:

🇯🇵 Board of Audit Findings

"The possibility of differences arising from price negotiations cannot be completely ruled out. Continued effort is needed to encourage the U.S. Government to make LOA (Letter of Offer and Acceptance) descriptions more detailed, to ensure price transparency."

— Source: Board of Audit FY2018 Diet-Requested Report, Chapter 3
🔗 📍 View Chapter 3 (Japanese)

In other words, "there is an explanation, but whether the differential is justified cannot be verified." This is the Board of Audit's effective position. Note: yen-converted figures above use May 2026 rates as a reference; they do not represent the actual yen amounts spent at the time of contract.

The same structure observed in Article #2's Tomahawk pricing (1.9–2.3× the U.S. Navy's domestic price) recurs here in F-35A procurement.

6. Bilateral on Paper, Multilateral Bundled Procurement in Practice

Another structural reason settlements take so long is multilateral bundled procurement. While the official framing of FMS is "bilateral, between the U.S. Government and the purchasing country government," the actual purchase contracts the U.S. Government concludes with weapon manufacturers often bundle orders from multiple countries. The Board of Audit makes this explicit:

🇯🇵 Board of Audit FY2018 Diet-Requested Report, Chapter 2

"In cases where the U.S. Government bundles the procurement of defense articles to be provided to multiple purchasing countries under FMS into a single purchase contract, even if one purchasing country has received full delivery of its defense articles, that country cannot receive the final settlement statement and is unable to receive the return of surplus funds for an extended period."

— Source: Board of Audit FY2018 Diet-Requested Report, Chapter 2
🔗 📍 View Chapter 2 (Japanese)

The F-35A illustrates the point. The U.S. Government's contract with Lockheed Martin for "Lot XX, X aircraft of F-35A" bundles orders from Japan, Australia, the U.K., Israel, Norway, and others. Even after Japan's allocation is fully delivered, the overall U.S.-Lockheed contract cannot be settled until other countries' allocations are completed. As a result, Japan never receives its final settlement statement, and any overpayment is not refunded.

The 1954 U.S.-Japan MDA Agreement is, on paper, a bilateral framework. But 2020s FMS practice has effectively become multilateral bundled procurement. The gap between formal structure and operational reality is one of the structural factors that supports the unresolved-settlement problem.

7. FY2023 — A Single Year of ¥123.9 Billion in Foreign Exchange Losses

The Board of Audit's FY2023 Settlement Audit Report visualized another FMS-related problem: foreign exchange losses. Because FMS contracts are denominated in U.S. dollars, the gap between the exchange rate at contract signing and at payment translates directly into cost. As the yen weakens, the same dollar-denominated contract requires more yen.

The Board of Audit's FY2023 report estimates the 5-year differential for the Ministry of Defense's FMS Central Procurement:

FY2019$3.96B¥453.3B¥436.2B▲17.1B

FY2022$3.78B¥416.1B¥407.7B▲8.3B
FY2023$5.79B¥668.8B¥792.8B+¥123.9B

Source: Board of Audit FY2023 Settlement Audit Report, Section 6 "Status of Defense Budget Execution."
🔗 📍 FY2023 Settlement Audit Report TOC

Between FY2019 and FY2022, the yen actually strengthened from contract to payment, so Japan paid less in yen than budgeted (savings of ¥2.7B–17.1B per year). In FY2023, rapid yen depreciation reversed this: the payment-period rate (around JPY 137/USD) was significantly higher than the contract-period rate (around JPY 108/USD).

The Board of Audit wrote:

🇯🇵 Board of Audit FY2023 Settlement Audit Report, Section 6

"The amount subject to payment in FY2023 was approximately $5.787 billion. The amount the Ministry of Defense decided to disburse for this, using the FY2023 disbursement officer's rate, was a total of ¥792.8 billion. By contrast, if this amount had been calculated using the disbursement officer's rates of the respective contract years, it would have been ¥668.8 billion. The difference of ¥123.9 billion can be seen as a loss arising from exchange-rate variation."

— Source: Board of Audit FY2023 Settlement Audit Report, Section 6
🔗 📍 FY2023 Audit Report TOC

"A loss arising from exchange-rate variation" — meaning that FY2023 alone required an additional ¥123.9 billion due to yen depreciation. This stems directly from FMS's prepayment structure. With years between contract and payment/delivery, exchange-rate risk is unilaterally borne by Japan.

⚠️ Note Japan's Ministry of Defense has introduced forward exchange contracts for some procurement to hedge against currency risk. However, in the case of FMS, the U.S. Government's billing schedule is fluid (settlement delays due to multilateral bundled procurement, variability in delivery timing, etc.), making it structurally difficult to apply forward contracts to the full contract amount. As a result, in periods of rapid yen depreciation like FY2023, large foreign exchange losses arise from amounts not covered by hedging. Exchange-rate risk allocation is not simply "Japan bears all of it unilaterally," but FMS's structure (prepayment + delivery delay + multilateral bundled procurement) makes exchange-rate hedging structurally difficult.

The same report also documents the trend in defense procurement future-year liability (amounts the government has committed to pay in future fiscal years):

From about ¥4.7 trillion at FY2019 year-end to about ¥9.46 trillion at FY2023 year-end — roughly doubled in 5 years. While Article #2's "one-time scheme" (the ¥1.84 trillion transfer from special accounts via the Defense Funding Securing Act) was indeed one-time, the underlying defense procurement is continuously expanding through the future-year liability mechanism.

8. Even the U.S. Side Doesn't Know — GAO Has Been Flagging This Since 1979

The same problem has been recognized on the U.S. side as well. The U.S. Government Accountability Office (GAO) is the investigative arm of the U.S. Congress, equivalent in standing to Japan's Board of Audit. In 1979, the GAO reported to Congress that the Navy's FMS Trust Fund balance was "unknown."

From GAO's 1979 finding to Japan's Board of Audit's first FMS flag in 1997: 18 years. From Japan's 1997 finding to the publication of this article in 2026: 29 years. Both countries' audit institutions have flagged the problem, yet the structure does not change. This is not a failure of individual case management; it is the more natural reading that the FMS system itself is designed in a way that can absorb audit findings from both nations without structural reform.

9. What Can and Cannot Be Confirmed from Public Sources

The primary sources used in this article — Japan's Board of Audit reports, the U.S. DSCA Historical Sales Book, the U.S. GAO report — are all publicly accessible. From them, the following can be confirmed:

  • Japan's cumulative FMS (FY1950–FY2024) is approximately $69.6 billion, ≈ ¥10.93 trillion at May 2026 reference rate
  • For roughly 30 years (29 years at writing) since FY1997, unresolved-settlement issues have been continuously flagged by the Board of Audit
  • FY2013–FY2017 unresolved settlements stayed in the ¥90–122 billion range annually
  • Prepayments are partially invested in U.S. Treasury securities
  • Interest accrues in the 0QQ account on the U.S. side; refund requires Japanese-side action
  • Multilateral bundled procurement means even bilateral transactions cannot settle until other countries' deliveries are complete
  • F-35A unit-price differences between Japan and the U.S. were in the $40–56 million range
  • In FY2023 alone, foreign exchange losses reached ¥123.9 billion
  • Future-year defense liabilities roughly doubled in 5 years

However, the following cannot be confirmed from public sources alone:

  • Annual balance and interest details of the 0QQ account (U.S. Treasury internal data)
  • Annual movements in the 7QQ account (post-settlement residual fund management)
  • Case-by-case breakdown of reasons for unresolved status (Japan's Ministry of Defense internal records)
  • Specifics of price negotiation history with the U.S. side (diplomatic documents)
  • Detailed minutes of U.S.-Japan SCCM (Security Cooperation Case Management) joint meetings (only summaries are public)

These are subject to disclosure requests. As a follow-up to this article, we plan to file freedom-of-information requests to Japan's Ministry of Defense, Ministry of Foreign Affairs, and Ministry of Finance sequentially.

10. Conclusion

Article #1 (¥80 trillion U.S. investment) and Article #2 (the ¥1.84 trillion defense funding scheme) addressed public capital that is about to flow, or is being structurally incorporated, from Japan to the United States. This article addressed the prior question: what has happened to the money already transferred?

The conclusions, in three points:

First, the structure has not changed for over a quarter of a century. Since Japan joined the Accelerated Case Closure Procedures in 1997, and the Board of Audit's first FMS flag in the same year, the same problem has been raised again and again. The Ministry of Defense compiled a Project Team report in 2008 and set improvement targets. SCCM working-level meetings with the U.S. side have been held. But the absolute level of unresolved settlements has not declined, foreign exchange losses have newly emerged, and future-year liabilities have expanded. This is not individual administrative failure; it is more naturally read as a system designed to absorb audit findings without structural reform.

Second, the money is not merely flowing — it is being managed and invested. Dollars that Japan prepays serve simultaneously as weapon-manufacturing funds and as U.S. Treasury securities investment funds. Interest accrues in the 0QQ account and is not returned unless Japan actively requests it. There is a gap between the official FMS framing ("the U.S. Government does not profit") and the operational reality. In private-sector terms, this is closer to: "Wire the full amount up front to the vendor's account; the vendor uses only what's needed for parts initially and parks the rest elsewhere, keeping the investment interest."

Third, audit findings from both countries have not stopped the structure. The U.S. GAO has flagged the FMS Trust Fund balance problem since 1979. Japan's Board of Audit has flagged unresolved-settlement and price-transparency issues since 1997. Both nations' audit institutions have raised these issues in parallel, yet FMS's fundamental machinery — full prepayment, management inside the U.S. Treasury, multilateral bundled procurement, multi-year delays in delivery and settlement — remains intact. This indicates that the system's design has the structural strength to treat audit findings as processable externalities.

This article reconstructs that structure using only primary sources. Readers can directly consult all of the cited Board of Audit reports, DSCA publications, and GAO reports — all freely accessible. The text above need not be taken on the editorial's authority. Go to the original sources and decode the structure for yourself. That is this site's purpose.
