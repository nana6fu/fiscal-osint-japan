1. The origin of "¥1,300 trillion debt"

"Japan's national debt is ¥1,300 trillion," "Every citizen owes ¥10 million" — these phrases are everywhere in Japanese news and political speech. Let's verify the source.

The Ministry of Finance (MOF) publishes "Outstanding Government Bonds and Borrowings" quarterly. As of end of FY2024 (March 31, 2025):

📄 Primary Source MOF "Outstanding Government Bonds, Borrowings, and Government Guarantees"
https://www.mof.go.jp/jgbs/reference/gbb/index.htm
ItemBalance (¥T)Nature
Ordinary government bonds~1,054Construction, deficit-financing, etc.
FILP bonds~97Fiscal Investment & Loan Program funds
Reconstruction bonds~4Post-2011 disaster reconstruction
Borrowings~53From local govts, banks, etc.
Government short-term securities~87Forex, short-term funding
Total~¥1,295T—

This is the source of "¥1,300 trillion debt." But there are three caveats:

  1. This is the central government's debt only — excludes local governments (~¥190T) and government-affiliated agencies. Also includes FILP bonds (~¥97T) where corresponding assets (loans) exist.
  2. Maturity and interest structure invisible — Some bonds mature in 10+ years, others in months. The actual repayment schedule isn't captured by this single number.
  3. This is the liability side only — No assets are considered. In corporate finance, no analyst declares "crisis" by looking only at liabilities.

What assets does the government have? That's what the next chapter addresses.

2. What is the MOF's "Financial Statements of the Government"?

Many readers may be surprised that the MOF publishes a "national balance sheet." It does so every year, though without much publicity.

📄 Primary Source MOF "Financial Statements of the Government of Japan (by ministry, general account & special accounts)"
https://www.mof.go.jp/policy/budget/report/public_finance_fact_sheet/index.html

The MOF's own FAQ states:

The Ministry of Finance prepares and publishes "Financial Statements of the Government of Japan," utilizing corporate accounting concepts to disclose the government's full flow and stock financial position. Source: MOF FAQ

This has been prepared annually since FY2003 — over 20 years of disclosure.

2.1 Connection to Takahashi Yoichi

Interestingly, Takahashi Yoichi (Professor at Kaetsu University, former MOF official) has been credited with creating the original framework. On the TV program "Seigi no Mikata" (May 21, 2022), he was introduced as "the person who, 25 years ago, was the first in the world to create a national balance sheet."

So the MOF's "Financial Statements" is, possibly, the work of the same Takahashi who now argues the "consolidated government" thesis we'll discuss in Chapter 5. This historical context shapes the debate.

The MOF also publishes a "Consolidated Financial Statements" separately, covering a broader scope.

📄 Primary Source MOF "FY2023 Consolidated Financial Statements" (Press Release, March 28, 2025)
https://www.mof.go.jp/policy/budget/report/public_finance_fact_sheet/fy2023/20250328houdouhappyou.html

Layer 2: Consolidated Financial Statements

ItemAmount (¥T)
Total Assets1,048.9

Notable: the difference improves from ▲¥695.7T (Layer 1) to ▲¥527.9T — improvement of ¥167.9 trillion. This is because independent administrative agencies (e.g., GPIF, Japan's pension reserve fund with ~¥277T) are now consolidated.

However, two important caveats:

  • GPIF's ¥277T is the citizens' pension reserve. It cannot legally or politically be diverted to debt repayment. It's "someone else's money" in the balance sheet.
  • The Bank of Japan is NOT included. This is what Takahashi Yoichi disputes — see next chapter.

5. The "Consolidated Government Balance Sheet" thesis (Takahashi Yoichi)

Takahashi Yoichi's argument is laid out in his 2019 book "Exposing the Fiscal Collapse Lie — Through the Consolidated Government Balance Sheet" (Heibonsha).

The MOF claims "Japan's finances will collapse," focusing only on outstanding government bonds. But naturally, a country has both debts and assets. Just as we view corporate finance on a consolidated basis, the national fiscal position must also be viewed as a "consolidated government" — with the Bank of Japan treated as a government subsidiary. Source: Takahashi Yoichi, 2019

5.1 Three-step logic

  1. BOJ as government subsidiary — BOJ holds ~¥500T of JGBs. As a special legal entity with government capital, it should be consolidated.
  2. Offset liabilities and assets internally — Government's JGB liability and BOJ's JGB asset cancel out on consolidation, as inter-company transactions would in corporate accounting.
  3. BOJ current account deposits are "not real debt" — Takahashi argues these are: (a) non-interest-bearing in principle, (b) backed by money-issuing authority, (c) similar in nature to banknotes (which no one demands repayment of).
🟡 Core claim (Takahashi):
Consolidate government + BOJ → JGBs held by BOJ cancel out → If BOJ current account deposits aren't "real debt" → Net position is roughly zero or even surplus → No need for tax hikes.

6. Counter-argument: The BOJ current account deposit problem

Two prominent economists challenge Takahashi's thesis: Tanaka Hideaki (Meiji University, former MOF) and Ikeda Nobuo (economist, former NHK).

6.1 Tanaka: Current account deposits do carry interest

📄 Primary Source Tanaka Hideaki, "Are there no risks to BOJ's bond purchases? An open question to Prof. Takahashi"
DIAMOND Online, May 2017
https://diamond.jp/articles/-/114027

Tanaka's argument:

  • BOJ current account deposits do receive interest (complementary deposit facility)
  • In an inflationary environment, rates must rise — increasing BOJ's interest expense
  • Therefore, the "non-interest-bearing forever" premise doesn't hold in reality
  • These deposits function as real debt

6.2 Tanaka: This would amount to 100% taxation

Even if you can offset the JGBs held by BOJ on a consolidated basis, the deposits held by private banks at BOJ remain as liabilities. If the government were to forcibly write these off to reduce its debt, that would be equivalent to seizing citizens' savings — i.e., 100% taxation on those deposits. Source: Tanaka Hideaki (paraphrased)

6.3 Ikeda: The liability just relocates

📄 Primary Source Ikeda Nobuo, "Consolidated government in ¥540 trillion 'deficit'" Agora, May 2022 (reposted 2024)
https://agora-web.jp/archives/220523063634.html
The reason Takahashi's balance sheet doesn't actually balance is that he excludes BOJ current account deposits from the liability side. The ¥540 trillion in JGBs is financed by current account deposits, so on a consolidated basis they appear on the liability side at the same magnitude. Even if all JGBs are absorbed by BOJ, the consolidated government's debt doesn't vanish — it just shifts to BOJ's debt to commercial banks. Source: Ikeda Nobuo, 2022

7. [Original Analysis] Both sides ignore the quality of assets

Here's an observation: both Takahashi and his critics are arguing about the liability side.

  • Takahashi: "BOJ deposits are not real debt" → reducing the liability
  • Tanaka, Ikeda: "Yes they are" → keeping the liability

But neither asks deeply about the asset side. Are the ¥778T in assets actually convertible to repayment funds?

7.1 The corporate analogy

Imagine a corporation with assets of ¥10 billion: HQ building ¥8B, private jet ¥1B, cafeteria ¥0.5B, cash ¥0.5B. Could it repay a ¥12B loan by selling these? Realistically — only the ¥0.5B cash. Selling HQ collapses the business.

For Japan's national balance sheet, the same question applies:

  • Can the Imperial Palace be sold? — Legally prohibited
  • Can Self-Defense Force bases be sold? — Strategically impossible
  • Can the National Diet building be sold? — Functionally impossible
  • Can national highways be sold? — Practically infeasible
  • Can dams and ports be sold? — Public infrastructure must continue operating

These ARE on the balance sheet as "assets," but realistically they cannot be sold for debt repayment.

7.2 The 24-year-old precursor: Ishii Kōki

This challenge to the "asset side" actually has a Japanese precursor from 2002: the late Ishii Kōki (then Member of the House of Representatives, Democratic Party of Japan).

📄 Primary Source Ishii Kōki, "Daremo Shiranai Nihonkoku no Urachōbo" (Japan's Hidden Books That No One Knows), 道出版 / Michi Publishing, 2002
CiNii bibliographic reference: https://ci.nii.ac.jp/ncid/BA58426555
National Diet Library / Diet record references for 154th session questions (2002)

In 2002, Ishii published "Japan's Hidden Books That No One Knows", presenting an independent analysis of Japan's fiscal reality:

  • "Special accounts are the real primary budget" — Behind the publicized ¥100T General Account lies ¥300–350T in Special Accounts
  • Assets of public corporations and quasi-governmental entities are booked at "book value" — vastly diverging from real-world market value
  • "Assets" tied to roads, dams, and public corporations cannot serve as collateral for debt repayment
  • These flow within a structure of bureaucratic-corporate interest that escapes democratic oversight

In the 154th Diet session (2002)

Ishii calculated, by removing duplications between the General Account, Special Accounts, and Fiscal Investment and Loan Program, that Japan's actual annual outlays approached ¥200 trillion — far above what most citizens understood. Then-Finance Minister Shiokawa Masajūrō responded:

You have stated your view, so I have no grounds to deny it. Source: Finance Minister Shiokawa Masajūrō, 154th Diet, 2002

The Finance Minister himself did not refute Ishii's analysis directly.

Connection to this article's analysis

The "liquidity axis" analysis developed in Chapters 8–9 below is not a sudden idea. It builds on Ishii's 24-year-old line of inquiry — a question of the asset side that was raised early, but never fully resolved in subsequent fiscal debates.

10. Is "no default because of sovereign currency" really true?

Up to here, we've discussed stocks (balance sheet). The final chapter discusses flow — sustainability.

Defenders of Japan's fiscal status often cite: "Japan can't default because the debt is in yen, which the BOJ can print." Is this true?

10.1 Formally — yes

JGBs are denominated in yen. The BOJ can issue yen. So technically, Japan cannot run out of money to repay. This is structurally different from Greece (euro-denominated) or Argentina (dollar-denominated debt). The technical default is impossible.

10.2 But three practical limits

Limit 1: Inflation

Printing money dilutes its value. Examples: Turkey (80%+ inflation 2022), Argentina (200%+), Zimbabwe (hyperinflation). "No technical default" ≠ "citizens are not impoverished."

Japan experienced inflation in 2022–2024 from imported costs. Real wages declined for a record 26 consecutive months (April 2022 onward), briefly turned positive due to one-time tax cut effect, then resumed declines.

Limit 2: Interest rates and BOJ's finances

BOJ holds ~¥500T in JGBs (yield: 0.291%). Commercial banks hold ~¥500T+ in BOJ current account deposits.

📄 Primary Source Daiwa Institute of Research, "Increasing interest expenses from BOJ rate hikes", January 24, 2025
https://www.dir.co.jp/report/research/capital-mkt/securities/20250124_024881.html
In the first half of FY2024, the BOJ's complementary deposit facility interest payments reached ¥392.2 billion — approximately 4 times the previous year's level. A further 0.25 percentage point rate hike would bring the deposit rate to 0.5%, exceeding the long-term JGB yield of 0.291%. Source: Daiwa Institute, January 2025
📌 Current real-time risk
BOJ ended negative interest rates in March 2024, raised rates to 0.25% in July 2024:
• Complementary deposit facility interest payments quadrupled year-over-year
• Spread between JGB yield (0.291%) and deposit rate narrowed
• Further hikes risk pushing BOJ into negative carry, losses, or insolvency

Takahashi's premise that "BOJ deposits are forever non-interest-bearing" is being tested by reality as of 2024 onward.

Limit 3: Currency value and living standards

Even if "Japan can't default," continued yen weakness directly impacts living standards. Section 10.3 examines this through the lens of the "1USD = 300 yen" thesis.

10.3 The "1USD = ¥300" claim — verified

Two prominent figures have endorsed extreme yen weakness as desirable:

📄 Primary Source Chunichi Sports, "Former PM Abe: '1USD=¥300 means rapid economic recovery'", May 2024
https://www.chunichi.co.jp/article/892925
Original statement: BS-TBS "Hodo 1930" April 25, 2024, citing Abe's April 2022 LDP Fiscal Policy Committee remarks
Former PM Abe (April 2022): "Employment gains are undeniably due to yen weakness. If 1USD=¥300, a Toyota car sells abroad for 1/3 the price. Japanese products become 1/3 the price. Japan tourism becomes 1/3 the price. The economy would rapidly recover."
Takahashi Yoichi (May 4, 2024, ABC TV "Seigi no Mikata"): "Even at 1USD=¥300, if the government returns its profits to citizens, no one would complain. Yen weakness — bring it on. At 1USD=¥300, growth rate would be 20%. The government is the biggest beneficiary of yen weakness."

The premise behind these claims:

  1. Japan is an export-dependent country → weak yen makes exports cheaper
  2. Government holds massive forex reserves (~¥160T) → weak yen creates unrealized gains
  3. Government can return these gains to citizens

10.4 "Beggar-thy-neighbor" theory and reality

Takahashi often invokes "beggar-thy-neighbor" policy — using weak currency to gain export competitiveness at neighbors' expense. He cites OECD models showing a 10% yen depreciation yields 0.4-1.2% GDP gain over 1-3 years.

True in theory. But here is what 5 datasets show for modern Japan:

Evidence 1: Real wages declining

📄 Primary Source Sumitomo Mitsui DS Asset Management, March 2024 / MHLW Monthly Labour Statistics

Japan's real wages declined for 26 consecutive months from April 2022. After a brief positive blip due to one-off tax effects in summer 2024, declines resumed. As of April 2025, real wages decreased 1.8% year-over-year — 4 consecutive months of contraction.

Evidence 2: Real GDP barely growing

Nomura Research Institute reports 2024 real GDP growth at +0.6%, down from +2.0% in 2023. "Headwinds from inflation and declining real wages dampened consumer spending."

Evidence 3: Offshore production ratio above 36%

📄 Primary Source JBIC, "FY2024 Overseas Direct Investment Survey (36th)"
https://www.jbic.go.jp/ja/information/press/press-2024/image/000009460.pdf

FY2023 manufacturing offshore production ratio: 36.0% (auto: 48.3%, electronics: 40.8%, textile: 59.4%). FY2024 forecast: 36.2%. Even with yen weakness, factories are NOT returning to Japan.

Evidence 4: METI itself acknowledges the model shift

📄 Primary Source METI Manufacturing Industries Bureau, "Current State and Challenges of Manufacturing Industry," May 2024
https://www.meti.go.jp/shingikai/sankoshin/seizo_sangyo/pdf/016_04_00.pdf
The weight has shifted from a "produce domestically, export abroad" trade-export model to a "produce locally, sell locally" local-production model. METI Manufacturing Industries Bureau, May 2024

The Japanese government itself acknowledges the end of the export-nation model.

Evidence 5: Household savings rate rising (consumer restraint)

Cabinet Office mini-paper (Feb 2025): Household savings rate rose to 4.4% (Q2 2024) and 3.9% (Q3 2024) — above the 2.9% pre-COVID baseline. "Despite increased dual-income household earnings, inflation and longevity concerns lead consumers to save rather than spend."

📌 Why "beggar-thy-neighbor" doesn't work in modern Japan
① Real wages declining → citizens' purchasing power falling
② Real GDP barely growing (+0.6%)
③ 36% offshore production → weak yen doesn't bring jobs home
④ METI acknowledges export-model is obsolete
⑤ Savings rate rising → consumers in defensive mode

The 1980-2000s "export-nation" Japan was a different country.
2010s onward Japan is an "investment-income nation" — beggar-thy-neighbor is misapplied.

10.5 Japan as an "investment-income nation"

📄 Primary Source METI "Trade White Paper 2025" Section 1: Balance of Payments
https://www.meti.go.jp/report/tsuhaku2025/2025honbun/i2310000.html

2024 Japan balance of payments:

  • Current account: +¥29.3T (record high)
  • Primary income: +¥40.2T (investment income, also record)
  • Trade balance: deficit
  • Services: deficit

Japan earns surplus through investment income, NOT through trade exports. As Pictet notes: "Japanese firms have shifted to local production, structurally distancing themselves from currency effects. The trade deficit, born from substituting exports with local production, has transformed into a primary income surplus."

But of the ¥40.2T primary income, ¥12.1T (about 30%) is "reinvested earnings" — held in overseas subsidiaries, NOT returning to Japan. Macro statistics show surplus, but it doesn't flow to domestic consumers.

By any fair-rate measure, 1USD=¥300 represents roughly 2× the fair value.

If 1USD=¥300 actually materialized:
• Oil and LNG import prices double in yen terms
• Wheat, soybeans, corn double
• Smartphones, PCs, semiconductors, pharmaceuticals double
• Japan's energy self-sufficiency: ~12%, food self-sufficiency: ~38% (calorie basis)

Takahashi argues the government could "return forex gains to citizens." But there is no historical precedent in postwar Japan of forex reserve gains being directly redistributed to citizens at the scale of ¥400T.
